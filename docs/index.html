Executive Summary

Large Language Models (LLMs) have transformed artificial intelligence, demonstrating near-human performance across language understanding, reasoning, and generation tasks. This report surveys the state of LLMs as of 2024, examining architectural innovations, benchmark performance, multimodal extensions, safety challenges, and the open versus closed-source ecosystem divide. Key findings suggest that while frontier models continue to push capability boundaries, the field is simultaneously grappling with alignment, interpretability, and efficiency challenges that will define the next phase of development.

Background

The modern LLM era began with the 2017 publication of Attention Is All You Need [1], which introduced the transformer architecture. Subsequent scaling work demonstrated that model performance improves predictably with more parameters and training data — a relationship formalised as neural scaling laws [2]. GPT-3 (175B parameters, 2020) demonstrated few-shot learning, while InstructGPT and ChatGPT (2022) showed that reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) could dramatically improve alignment and helpfulness [3].

Key Findings

Frontier Model Performance

GPT-4 (2023) achieved 86.4% on the MMLU benchmark (measuring knowledge across 57 subjects), surpassing human expert performance in many categories [4]. Google's Gemini Ultra matched or exceeded GPT-4 on several benchmarks, including a reported 90.0% on MMLU [5]. Meta's LLaMA 2 (70B parameters) achieved 75.2% MMLU — remarkable for an open-weight model [6].

Multimodal LLMs

The field has rapidly expanded beyond text. GPT-4V, LLaVA [7], and Gemini Ultra natively process images alongside text. Audio integration (Whisper, speech-to-speech models) and video understanding are active research frontiers. These capabilities open new applications in medical imaging analysis, visual question answering, and document understanding.

Open vs Closed Source

A significant divide has emerged. Closed models (GPT-4, Claude 3, Gemini) offer the highest capabilities but limit researcher access. Open-weight models (LLaMA 2, Mistral 7B, Falcon) enable community research, fine-tuning, and on-premise deployment, but lag slightly in raw capability. The release of LLaMA 2 by Meta democratised high-quality LLM research and sparked a proliferation of fine-tuned variants [8].

Safety and Alignment

Aligning LLM behaviour with human values remains a central challenge. RLHF, Direct Preference Optimisation (DPO), and Constitutional AI are the dominant approaches [9]. Despite progress, models still exhibit hallucinations, sycophancy, and susceptibility to adversarial prompting. Red-teaming and automated evaluation frameworks are now standard practice at major labs.

Analysis

The capability gains from scaling appear to be slowing at the frontier without architectural or training-data innovations. Efficient inference — quantisation, speculative decoding, mixture-of-experts routing — has become as important as raw parameter count. The economic implications are significant: inference costs at GPT-4 scale are non-trivial, creating commercial pressure toward smaller, faster models.

The open vs closed debate also has geopolitical dimensions, with export controls on advanced AI chips and competing national AI strategies influencing which organisations can access frontier training compute.

Conclusion

Near-term developments likely include: (1) widespread adoption of 7B–13B parameter models on consumer hardware; (2) improved tool use and agentic capabilities; (3) better multimodal integration; (4) more robust alignment techniques reducing hallucination rates. The central challenge for the field is not raw capability but reliable, safe, and efficient deployment at scale.
